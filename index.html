Ultimate Key To Success

Adverb / Verb Combination

Positive / Endorsement

Use when the writer validates, supports, encourages or approves.

  • Warmly celebrates
  • Gently reinforces
  • Enthusiastically endorses
  • Passionately supports
  • Wholeheartedly champions

Negative / Critical

Use when the writer attacks, rejects, discredits or warns.

  • Scathingly critiques
  • Scornfully condemns
  • Fiercely denounces
  • Bluntly dismisses
  • Harshly rebukes

Neutral / Measured

Use when the writer explains, observes, frames or reasons.

  • Calmly contends
  • Firmly asserts
  • Logically reasons
  • Objectively notes
  • Measuredly argues

Satirical / Ironic

Use when the writer mocks, ridicules or exposes absurdity.

  • Sardonically mocks
  • Mockingly ridicules
  • Wryly observes
  • Sarcastically derides
  • Ironically exposes

Sentence Starters

Analysis Openers

  • By employing …, the author …
  • Through the deliberately … depiction of …, the author …
  • By characterising … as …, the author …
  • The author’s use of … enables them to …
  • In framing … as …, the author …

Audience Effect Openers

  • Such language positions … to …
  • Accordingly, … may feel compelled to …
  • This encourages … to perceive … as …
  • As a consequence, … are invited to …
  • Thereby, … are predisposed to …

Introduction · 4 Steps

  • (1) Broader Implications
  • (2) Contention
  • (3) Audience and Image
  • (4) Call to Action

Ubiquitous within modern society lies the need to…, bringing into question whether …

In light of this, John Wick (adverb) contends that …. As it poses… within his article entitled “…”.

Appealing directly towards …, Wick supplements his piece with an image of ... in order to substantiate his piece.

Ultimately, Wick (adverb) calls for individuals to (contention) … as only then can …

Example

Ubiquitous within modern society lies the need for students to remain focused within learning environments, bringing into question whether mobile phones should be restricted during class. In light of this, Sarah Miller firmly contends that mobile phones should be restricted during class as they pose a significant distraction to students within her opinion piece entitled “Put the Phones Away”. Appealing directly towards parents, teachers and students, Miller supplements her piece with an image of a student distracted by their phone during class in order to substantiate her piece. Ultimately, Miller strongly calls for schools to restrict mobile-phone use during class as only then can students remain focused and engaged in their learning.

Shepherding

Function: orient the audience, establish shared values and guide them toward the author’s preferred lens.

Sample Topic Sentences

From the outset, [author] warmly constructs … as …, inviting [audience] to identify with … before confronting them with …

Initially, [author] begins with a community-based argument, evoking … in order to predispose [audience] to …

What to Analyse

  • Shared values and assumptions.
  • Community identity and belonging.
  • Emotional predisposition before the central argument develops.
  • How the opening frames the issue.

Substantiating

Function: validate the contention with evidence, examples, anecdotes, statistics, expert authority or logical reasoning.

Useful analytical move: explain not simply that evidence “proves” the author’s point, but why the selected evidence appears credible, representative or difficult for the target audience to dismiss.

Mobilising

Function: convert audience agreement into a practical, emotional or behavioural response.

Personal Responsibility Frames action as an individual obligation rather than something that can be delegated to others.

By directly implicating [audience] in …, [author] seeks to dismantle the comfort of passive observation and make inaction itself appear consequential.

Collective Responsibility Positions the issue as dependent on shared community action.

Through inclusive language such as “we”, “our” and “together”, [author] collapses the distance between writer and audience, framing the desired response as a communal obligation.

Practical Call to Action Gives the audience a clear behaviour they can realistically perform.

By translating the broader contention into the tangible action of …, [author] makes compliance appear achievable rather than abstract.

Conclusion

Ultimately, through [final persuasive strategy], [author] consolidates their portrayal of …, encouraging [specific audience] to …

A strong conclusion should synthesise the author’s final positioning of the audience rather than merely repeat the contention.

5 Steps of Analysis

  • 1 · Identify the Technique
  • 2 · Explain the Mechanism
  • 3 · Identify the Specific Audience
  • 4 · Explain the Audience Effect
  • 5 · Connect to the Argument

By employing [technique], [author] [mechanism], thereby positioning [specific audience] to [reaction / emotion] and ultimately encouraging them to [accept the relevant argument / act].

High-level analysis explains why this specific persuasive choice is strategically suited to this specific audience at this specific stage of the argument.

Common Types of Arguments

Problem → Consequence → Solution

Establish the issue, magnify its cost, then present the preferred action as necessary or sensible.

Shared Values → Threat → Response

Build identification, reveal what threatens those values, then mobilise the audience to protect them.

Misconception → Correction

Present an opposing assumption before dismantling it through reasoning or evidence.

Anecdote → Broader Pattern

Use an individual event to embody a wider social problem and increase immediacy.

Persuasive Technique Setups

Use the setup formulas below as flexible analytical frameworks. The strongest analysis adapts the wording to the specific argument, audience and context rather than reproducing a formula mechanically.

Alliteration

Setup: Through the deliberate alliteration of “[quote]”, [author] creates a memorable rhythmic emphasis around …

Push further: analyse what idea is made more memorable or emotionally charged by the sound pattern.

Anaphora

Setup: Through the anaphoric repetition of “[opening phrase]”, [author] rhythmically reinforces …

Push further: explain how repeated sentence openings accumulate urgency, certainty or emotional momentum.

Anecdotal Evidence

Setup: By recounting the personal experience of …, [author] humanises the broader issue of …

Push further: explain why this individual story may appear authentic, relatable or representative to the target audience.

Asyndeton

Setup: Through the asyndetic listing of “[terms]”, [author] accelerates the pace of the sentence and creates an impression of …

Auxesis

Setup: Through auxesis, [author] escalates from “[initial term]” to “[stronger term]”, progressively intensifying the perceived severity of …

Push further: identify the progression in force and explain how the climax shapes the audience’s sense of urgency.

Bold Word / Title

Setup: By visually foregrounding the term “[word/title]” through bold typography, [author] directs immediate attention toward …

Connotations

Setup: The term “[word]” carries connotations of …, enabling [author] to characterise … as …

Push further: name the precise associations activated by the word instead of merely calling the language “negative” or “positive”.

Dichotomy

Setup: By constructing a dichotomy between “[side one]” and “[side two]”, [author] narrows the audience’s perceived choices and privileges …

Image Analysis

Setup: By presenting an image of … [describe the image], the [author] seeks to position [specific audience] into … [feeling a certain way, emotion] since [what does the image represent]. Therefore, such [audience] are influenced to [specific response or action].

Example: By presenting an image of an exhausted student surrounded by unfinished schoolwork, the author seeks to position parents into feeling concerned for students’ wellbeing since the image represents the damaging consequences of excessive academic pressure. Therefore, such parents are influenced to support the author’s call for a more balanced workload.

Imagery Types

Visual Imagery

Creates a picture in the audience’s mind through sight-based description.

Auditory Imagery

Uses sound-based description to make an event feel immediate or confronting.

Tactile Imagery

Invokes touch, texture or bodily sensation to intensify physical immediacy.

Plosive

Setup: Through the repeated plosive consonants in “[quote]”, [author] creates a sharp, forceful sound that acoustically reinforces …

Push further: connect the sound quality to the author’s tone or characterisation rather than treating plosives as automatically “aggressive”.

Rhetorical Questions

Setup: By asking “[question]?”, [author] invites [audience] to internally supply the assumed answer that …

Push further: identify the assumption built into the question and explain how disagreement is made to appear unreasonable, uncomfortable or morally suspect.

Semantic Field

Setup: Through a semantic field of [concept], with the terms “[term]”, “[term]” and “[term]”, [author] constructs …

Push further: explain the cumulative conceptual pattern created by the related vocabulary.

Statistics

Setup: By citing the statistic “[data]”, [author] lends numerical authority to the claim that …

Syntactic Parallelism

Setup: Through syntactic parallelism in “[first clause] [second clause]”, [author] creates a balanced rhythmic structure that reinforces …

Push further: identify exactly what grammatical structure is repeated and why the parallel construction makes the paired ideas feel equivalent, cumulative or memorable.

Tonal Shift

Setup: [Author] shifts from a [initial tone] tone to a [later tone] tone, marking a strategic transition from … to …

Spoken / Audio Texts

Vocal Delivery

Analyse pace, stress, pause, volume, intonation and changes in vocal energy where they materially shape persuasion.

Sound and Music

Analyse how music, silence, sound effects or ambient noise frame the audience’s emotional response and interact with the spoken argument.

Audience Analysis

How to identify the specific audience: look for directly addressed groups, stakeholders affected by the issue, groups whose values are invoked, and people capable of performing the requested action.

Audience Values

What might this group value, fear, already believe or potentially overlook?

Audience Reaction

What precise emotion, judgment or behavioural response is the author attempting to create?

Common Types of Pieces

Opinion Piece

Often uses a personal voice, explicit contention and sustained persuasive argument.

Editorial

May speak with an institutional or publication-level voice and present a broader communal position.

Speech

Often uses direct address, repetition, inclusive pronouns and rhythmic structures suited to live delivery.

Final Checklist

  • Have I identified the author’s argument rather than merely listed techniques?
  • Have I specified the relevant audience instead of repeatedly writing “the reader”?
  • Have I explained how the language or visual actually produces the intended response?
  • Have I connected each analytical point to the progression of the argument?
  • Have I used precise evidence?
  • Have I varied my analytical verbs and sentence structures?
  • Have I avoided simply labelling language as positive, negative or persuasive?
